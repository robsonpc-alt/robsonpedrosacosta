Research

Research

Connected and comparative histories of slavery, women, religion, resistance, and dependency across Africa, Brazil, and the Atlantic World.

Current priority

Women, Slavery & African Diaspora

Current research focuses especially on African and Afro-descendant women and the connected histories that link Africa, Brazil, Latin America, the Caribbean, and the wider diaspora. Themes include slavery, resistance, freedom, motherhood, family, mobility, religion, reproductive experiences, and forms of social and political agency.

This agenda underpins the CNPq-funded project Women of Africa, Women of the Diaspora: Connections, Transgressions and Resistance, as well as emerging international networks and edited volumes.

Long-term research

Religion, Slavery & Resistance

A long-term strand of research examines Catholic institutions as slaveholders, especially the Benedictine Order, and explores institutional paternalism, moral regulation, labor regimes, emancipation, negotiation, resistance, and enslaved people’s religious and social worlds.

This work includes the Marie Skłodowska-Curie project SLAVESRELIGION – Slaves of Religion: Paternalism and Resistance, Brazil and the monograph Paternalism, Transgression and Slave Resistance in Brazil.

Specialized agenda

Slave-owning Slaves in Africa and Brazil

Another important research strand examines enslaved individuals who themselves owned enslaved people. These studies explore hierarchy, legal status, dependence, social mobility, agency, and the conceptual challenges posed by slave-owning slaves across Brazil and Africa.

Publications include studies of Nicolau de Souza and Rufina, the Brill chapter “Black Masters,” and two forthcoming articles: “Slaves-owning slaves: rethinking a concept” and “Slaves-owning slaves: a peculiar African institution.”

Methodological approach

Connected, comparative and transnational history

Across these themes, the research moves between individual trajectories, institutions, social relations, legal practices, and transatlantic connections. It combines microhistorical sensitivity with connected and comparative perspectives to examine how slavery, gender, religion, and dependence changed across different societies and historical contexts.
